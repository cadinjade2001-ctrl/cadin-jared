Why this matters right now

Every math class I have taught holds a wide range of learners. Students who read above grade level sit next to students who come to me every day for RTI. Most of us teach from district lessons that move fast and assume every student can keep up with the same page at the same time.

CAST released version 3.0 of the UDL Guidelines in 2024, with more focus on learner agency (CAST, 2024). Schools are talking about UDL more than ever, but in reality most teachers I know have never been shown how to apply it to the lesson they teach tomorrow.

UDL tells me where my lesson creates a barrier. Cognitive science tells me how to fix it.

The research on UDL is still being debated. One meta-analysis found significant positive effects (Almeqdad et al., 2023), while Boysen (2024) found that many studies CAST cites give little direct support for its claims. So I use UDL as a lens to find barriers, then match each one to a strategy with strong evidence: scaffolding that fades, managing cognitive load, and retrieval and spaced practice (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Sweller et al., 2019; van de Pol et al., 2010).

The 15 minute UDL lesson audit

You do not need a full curriculum review. Pick one lesson you are about to teach and walk through these five steps during a planning period. I built this from the audit I did on my own measurement unit, then simplified it so a grade level team can do it together.

  1. Pick one lesson and its target

    Write the "I can" statement at the top of your page. Everything you change has to keep this target the same for every student.

  2. Walk through it as three different students

    Picture a student who struggles to read or is learning English, a student who has trouble holding steps in memory or staying focused, and a student who struggles with writing or fine motor tasks. Go through the I Do, We Do, and You Do as each of them.

  3. Sort what you notice

    Put each note in one of three columns: strengths (what already works), barriers (what makes it harder for some students), and missing opportunities (what could be there but is not).

  4. Match each barrier to a checkpoint and a strategy

    Use the checklist below to name the UDL checkpoint, then use the strategy guide to pick one evidence-based fix.

  5. Make one change and plan when to take it away

    Choose the one barrier that affects the most students. Plan the support, and plan the day you will start fading it so students end up doing it on their own.

What I learned auditing my own measurement unit

By Cadin Jared · December 2026 · 4th grade math

I first tried this with the opening lessons of our district measurement module: metric length, customary length, and liquid volume (4.MD.A.1 and 4.MD.A.2). I teach these lessons to two classes of about 50 students total.

The lessons were already strong in engagement. Every lesson opens with a hook where students estimate before they measure, and the cooperative structures get everyone talking. The connections to tenths and fractions are excellent.

The barrier I had never noticed

Every lesson depends on one habit: figure out what each space on the tool is worth. During the I Do, that step is right there on the slide. Once the slide changes, it is gone. My students who struggle were trying to remember the steps, read tiny tick marks, and write a decimal all at the same time. That is a lot to hold in your head at once.

What I changed

A desk card with fading support. Every student gets the black and white benchmark card from the printables page. On day one we use it together on every problem. By day two partners use it. By day three students try first and only flip the card over when they get stuck. The card goes away for the test. This is scaffolding that fades, which is what the research says makes support work long term (van de Pol et al., 2010).

Vocabulary in smaller chunks. The liquid volume lesson introduces ten new words in one day, so I pre-teach them the day before in two groups with picture cards. Less new information at once means more room to actually think about the math (Sweller et al., 2019).

More than one way to show it. Students can show a measurement by writing it, drawing and labeling the ruler, or explaining it to me while I record. The target stays the same.

Quick self-rating. Students rate themselves 1 to 4 on the target after the I Do and again at closure, which also tells me who to pull for small group the next day.

What I would tell another teacher

None of these changes needed new materials beyond copy paper. Start with one lesson, find the one barrier that affects the most students, and plan when you will take the support away. If your team audits the first lessons of each module together, you only have to build supports like the benchmark card once.

Free printables

Both are designed for black and white printing. Print this page from your browser, or copy the text into your own template. Print the card front and back, fold, and tape it to the desk. No laminating needed.

My Benchmark Card (front)

Each small space on a centimeter ruler = 0.1 cm

  1. 1 mm · thickness of a dime
  2. 1 cm · width of a fingertip
  3. 1 m · width of a door
  4. 1 inch · thumb knuckle
  5. 1 foot · length of a shoe
  6. 1 yard · arm span
  7. My own benchmark: ______________

Measuring Steps (back)

  1. ☐ Did I start at 0? (If not, subtract the start.)
  2. ☐ What is each space worth?
  3. ☐ Which two numbers is it between?
  4. ☐ Which number is it closer to?
  5. ☐ Does it match my estimate?
  6. ☐ Did I write the unit?

Works for rulers, graduated cylinders, measuring cups, and scales.

Add a UDL check to your weekly plans

Most grade level teams already plan row by row: standard, target, I Do, We Do, You Do, and materials. Adding one more row makes the audit part of planning instead of something extra.

That moves a team from fixing problems for one student at a time to planning lessons where fewer students run into the problem at all.
