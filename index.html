Hi, I'm Cadin!

I teach 4th grade math and science in Clarksville, Tennessee. I made this site to share something that changed the way I plan my math lessons, and I hope it helps other teachers too.

My classroom has a tiger theme, and my math groups are all named after tigers. I also print everything in black and white, so that is how I made the printables on this site too!

Every math class I have taught has a wide range of learners. I have students who read above grade level sitting next to students who come to me every day for RTI. Some students need a checklist on their desk to get through the block, and some shut down when a page looks too long. Like a lot of teachers, I teach from district lessons that move fast and expect everyone to keep up with the same page at the same time.

Universal Design for Learning (UDL) is a way to plan for all of those students from the start instead of fixing a lesson after it doesn't work. Schools talk about UDL a lot, but in reality most teachers I know have never been shown how to actually use it on the lesson they are teaching tomorrow. That is what this site is for.

What's on this site

  • The 15 minute audit: five steps and a checklist to look at any math lesson in one planning period
  • Strategies: common barriers in the math block and what to do about them
  • My classroom: how I used this on my own measurement unit
  • Printables: a black and white desk card and checklist you can print

A quick note on the research

I use UDL to find where my lesson creates a barrier, then fix it with a strategy that has strong research behind it, like scaffolding or retrieval practice (CAST, 2024; Dunlosky et al., 2013). You'll see the research for each strategy on the Strategies page.

The 15 Minute Lesson Audit

You don't need to review your whole curriculum. Pick one lesson you are about to teach and go through these steps during a planning period. I came up with these after auditing my own measurement lessons for my master's program, and I kept them simple enough that a grade level team could do it together.

  1. Pick one lesson and write down the target. Whatever you change, the "I can" statement stays the same for every student.
  2. Go through the lesson as three different students. Picture a student who struggles to read or is learning English, a student who has a hard time remembering steps or staying focused, and a student who struggles with writing. Walk through your I Do, We Do, and You Do as each of them.
  3. Sort what you notice. Make three columns: strengths (what already works), barriers (what makes it harder for some students), and missing opportunities (what could be there but isn't).
  4. Match each barrier to a strategy. Use the checklist below to name the UDL checkpoint, then look at the strategies page for a fix.
  5. Make one change and plan when to take it away. Pick the barrier that affects the most students. Plan the support and plan the day you will start fading it.

Audit Checklist

Print this or keep it next to you while you plan. Check off what your lesson already does. Anything you leave unchecked is a place to look. The numbers match the checkpoints in the CAST (2024) guidelines.

What I Learned Auditing My Own Measurement Unit

December 2026

I first tried this on the first three lessons of our district measurement module: metric length, customary length, and liquid volume (4.MD.A.1 and 4.MD.A.2). I teach these lessons to my homeroom and my second block, so that's about 50 students.

Honestly, the lessons were already strong in a lot of ways. Every lesson starts with a hook where students estimate before they measure, and the Kagan structures get everyone talking. The connections to tenths and fractions are really good.

The barrier I never noticed

Every one of these lessons depends on one habit: figuring out what each space on the tool is worth. During the I Do, that step is right there on the slide. Once the slide changes, it's gone. My students who struggle were trying to remember the steps, read tiny tick marks, and write a decimal all at the same time. That is a lot to hold in your head.

What I changed

A desk card. Every student gets a black and white benchmark card (it's on the printables page). On day one we use it together on every problem. On day two they use it with a partner. By day three they try it on their own first and only flip the card over if they get stuck. The card goes away for the test.

Smaller chunks of vocabulary. The liquid volume lesson has ten new words in one day, so I pre-teach them the day before in two groups with picture cards.

More than one way to show it. Students can write the measurement, draw and label the ruler, or explain it to me while I write it down. The target stays the same.

A quick self-rating. Students rate themselves 1 to 4 on the target after the I Do and again at closure. It also tells me who to pull for small group the next day.

What I would tell another teacher

None of this needed new materials, just copy paper. Start with one lesson, find the barrier that affects the most kids, and plan when you will take the support away. If your team does the first lessons of each module together, you only have to make things like the desk card one time.
